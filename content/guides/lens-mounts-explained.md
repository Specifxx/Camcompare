The lens mount is the ring on the front of a camera where the lens locks on. It decides which lenses fit, which adapters work and which second-hand lenses you can use. You will probably replace the body after a few years, but the mount you pick shapes what you can buy for much longer. This guide explains how mounts differ, what adapting can and cannot do, and how to choose one.

## What a lens mount is

A mount is more than a bayonet. It does three jobs:

- **Mechanical.** It holds the lens at an exact distance and angle from the sensor, and carries its weight.
- **Electrical.** Contacts carry power and data. The camera uses them to drive autofocus, set the aperture, read the focal length for stabilisation and apply lens corrections.
- **Optical.** The size of the opening and its distance from the sensor set limits on what lens designers can build.

The electrical language the lens and body speak is usually the camera maker's own. Some makers share it with other lens brands; others keep it closed. That choice matters as much as the metal.

### Flange distance

Flange distance, or flange focal distance, is the gap between the face of the mount and the sensor. Every lens for a mount is built for that exact gap. If it is off, the lens will not focus correctly, and usually not at infinity.

DSLRs need a long flange distance because a mirror swings up between the lens and the sensor. Canon EF is 44 mm and Nikon F is 46.5 mm. Mirrorless cameras have no mirror, so the mount can sit much closer to the sensor: 16 to 20 mm for most of the systems below.

That one number explains most of what you can and cannot adapt.

### Throat diameter

The throat is the mount's inner opening. A wider throat gives lens designers more room, especially for very fast lenses. Nikon Z is 55 mm across, Canon RF 54 mm and Sony E 46.1 mm. A wider throat does not guarantee better lenses, though. Every current mount has sharp, fast lenses, and Sony makes f/1.2 lenses for its narrower E-mount.

## The current mirrorless mounts

### Sony E

Sony launched E-mount in 2010 on its APS-C NEX cameras and added full frame with the A7 and A7R in 2013. One mount serves both formats; Sony labels its full-frame lenses FE. In 2011 Sony published the mount's specification to other lens makers without charging a royalty. That is a large part of why E-mount has the widest range of third-party lenses.

### Canon RF

Canon introduced RF in 2018 with the full-frame EOS R. It has a 54 mm throat, a 20 mm flange distance and 12 electrical contacts. APS-C bodies and RF-S lenses followed in 2022. Canon is the strictest of the big makers about third-party autofocus lenses (see below). Its own EF-EOS R adapters, in plain, control-ring and drop-in filter versions, let EF and EF-S lenses work fully on RF bodies.

### Nikon Z

Nikon Z arrived in 2018 with the Z6 and Z7. It has the shortest flange distance (16 mm) and the widest throat (55 mm) of the full-frame mounts. It covers full frame (FX) and APS-C (DX). Nikon's FTZ and FTZ II adapters let many F-mount lenses work on Z bodies.

### Fujifilm X and G

Fujifilm runs two separate mounts. X-mount arrived in 2012 with the X-Pro1 and is APS-C only. G-mount, launched in 2016 for the GFX cameras, is medium format, with sensors of about 44 × 33 mm, a 26.7 mm flange distance and a 65 mm throat. X lenses do not fit G bodies, or the other way round.

### L-Mount

Leica introduced the mount in 2014 on the APS-C Leica T and later used it for the full-frame SL. In 2018 Leica, Panasonic and Sigma formed the L-Mount Alliance, so bodies and lenses from member brands share one mount. Later members include Leitz Cine, DJI, Samyang, Astrodesign, Blackmagic Design, Sirui, Viltrox and Freefly Systems. Not every member makes photo lenses; some make cinema cameras or lenses. Photo bodies come mainly from Leica, Panasonic's Lumix S range and Sigma.

### Micro Four Thirds

Olympus and Panasonic launched Micro Four Thirds in 2008; the first camera was the Panasonic G1. It is a shared standard, so Panasonic and OM System (the former Olympus camera business) lenses fit each other's bodies. The sensor is about 17.3 × 13 mm, with a crop factor of 2. Lenses, especially telephotos, are small for their reach. Some features, such as combined lens-and-body stabilisation, work only when lens and body come from the same brand.

## The legacy DSLR mounts

### Canon EF

Canon introduced EF in 1987. It is fully electronic: there is no mechanical focus or aperture linkage, and every autofocus lens has its own motor. EF-S lenses, for APS-C, fit only Canon's APS-C DSLRs, while EF lenses fit every EOS DSLR. Canon's last new EF lens design came out in 2018. Because the mount is all-electronic, EF lenses adapt better than any other DSLR lenses.

### Nikon F

Nikon has used the F bayonet since the Nikon F of 1959, adding metering, data contacts and autofocus along the way. That makes compatibility a patchwork:

- **AI and AI-S** lenses are manual focus with an aperture ring.
- **AF and AF-D** lenses are focused by a screw motor in the camera body. Nikon's entry-level DSLRs and all Z cameras lack that motor.
- **AF-S, AF-I and AF-P** lenses have the motor in the lens.
- **G** lenses have no aperture ring, and **E** lenses set the aperture electronically.

On a Z body with the FTZ or FTZ II, AF-S, AF-I and AF-P lenses autofocus. AF and AF-D lenses keep metering but must be focused by hand.

### Pentax K

Pentax introduced K-mount in 1975, and Ricoh still makes Pentax K-mount DSLRs today. K bodies accept lenses going back decades, though older lenses lose some automation. Many K lenses have aperture rings, so they also adapt easily to mirrorless bodies.

## Adapting lenses: what works and what does not

### The flange-distance rule

An adapter is mostly a precise spacer. That gives one firm rule:

- **Long to short works.** You can put a lens on a body with a shorter flange distance by filling the difference. EF lenses (44 mm) go on RF bodies (20 mm) through a 24 mm adapter.
- **Short to long does not.** Putting a lens on a body with a longer flange distance needs glass in the adapter, which costs light and image quality. Without it, the lens cannot focus to infinity and becomes close-up only.

So mirrorless lenses do not go on DSLRs. Canon's own EF-M lenses (18 mm) cannot go on RF (20 mm). Nikon Z lenses, with the shortest flange distance, cannot be adapted to any of the other mounts here. Between mirrorless mounts the gaps are tiny. Sony E is 2 mm longer than Nikon Z, and thin electronic adapters exist for that pairing, but most other mirrorless-to-mirrorless combinations have no room.

### Autofocus and electronics

The most reliable adapters come from the camera maker: Canon's EF-EOS R, Nikon's FTZ and FTZ II, and Sony's LA-EA adapters for its older A-mount lenses.

Cross-brand electronic adapters, such as Canon EF to Sony E, Fujifilm X, L-Mount or Nikon Z, translate one maker's language into another's, usually by reverse engineering. Results vary by lens and body. Autofocus can be slower, tracking less dependable, and a camera firmware update can break compatibility until the adapter maker issues a fix. Before buying one, check the adapter maker's compatibility list and whether its firmware can be updated. Sigma's MC-11 (EF or SA to E) and MC-21 (EF or SA to L) are designed around Sigma's own lenses.

### Manual lenses

Manual-focus lenses with an aperture ring are the easiest to adapt. A simple adapter with no electronics is enough, and focus peaking and magnification make focusing practical. On many bodies with in-body stabilisation, you enter the focal length by hand.

Lenses with no aperture ring are harder. Nikon G and many Pentax lenses set the aperture with a lever, so they need an adapter with its own aperture control. Canon EF and Nikon E lenses set it electronically and need an electronic adapter.

Leica M rangefinder lenses adapt to almost every mirrorless mount. Some wide-angle ones show colour casts or soft corners on digital sensors, so look for tests with your body.

### Sensor coverage and focal reducers

An APS-C lens on a full-frame body gives dark corners unless the camera crops. Canon, Nikon and Sony full-frame bodies crop automatically with their own APS-C lenses.

A full-frame lens on a smaller sensor fits but frames tighter. A 50 mm lens frames like about 75–80 mm on APS-C and 100 mm on Micro Four Thirds.

A focal reducer, sometimes sold as a speed booster, is an adapter with glass that shrinks the image. A 0.71× reducer brings a full-frame lens closer to its original field of view on APS-C and makes it about one stop brighter. Reducers only work from a larger format to a smaller one.

### A bridge, not a plan

Adapting is a good way to keep using lenses you already own while you move across, and a cheap way into manual lenses. As the backbone of a new system, expect compromises.

## Third-party lenses, mount by mount

Third-party support varies a lot, and it changes the cost of a system.

- **Sony E:** the widest choice. Sigma, Tamron, Samyang, Viltrox, Tokina, Zeiss, Voigtländer and others make autofocus lenses for full frame and APS-C.
- **Nikon Z:** Nikon licenses the mount to Sigma, Tamron and Cosina (Voigtländer). Other brands sell reverse-engineered autofocus lenses. The range is growing but smaller than E.
- **Canon RF:** Canon allows third-party manual-focus lenses and autofocus lenses that cover only APS-C. Sigma and Tamron make APS-C autofocus lenses. Earlier full-frame autofocus lenses from Samyang, Viltrox and Yongnuo were withdrawn at Canon's request.
- **Fujifilm X:** open to third parties. Sigma, Tamron, Viltrox, Tokina, Samyang and others make autofocus lenses.
- **Fujifilm G:** autofocus lenses are almost all Fujinon. Third-party lenses have been manual focus, with the first autofocus ones announced in 2026.
- **L-Mount:** Leica, Panasonic and Sigma lenses are all native, and Samyang, Sirui and Viltrox have joined. Sigma's L-Mount range is large.
- **Micro Four Thirds:** Panasonic and OM System lenses work across both brands, alongside Sigma and many manual-focus makers.
- **Canon EF and Nikon F:** decades of third-party lenses, now bought mostly second-hand.
- **Pentax K:** new third-party autofocus lenses are few. Most choice is older lenses on the used market.

## Why the mount matters more than the body

A camera body is the part you replace. Lenses usually stay, and they come with you when you upgrade within the same mount.

The mount decides which lenses you can buy now and later. Before you choose a body, list the two or three lenses you would buy first, plus the one you expect to want next: a long telephoto for wildlife, small primes for travel, fast primes for low light. Check that they exist on that mount, new or used, at prices you can pay. Our [lens listings](/lenses) and [camera finder](/finder) help with this.

Third-party access widens the price range. A mount with several lens makers usually gives you more options at each focal length than one that relies on its own brand.

Switching is costly. Moving systems means selling lenses at used prices and buying again. Mounts can also end: Canon EF-M, Samsung NX and Nikon 1 all stopped getting new bodies and lenses. A mount its maker is still developing is the safer bet.

Then choose the body. [Compare bodies](/compare) on the same mount and buy the one that suits you, knowing your lenses will outlast it.

## Buying lenses and adapters in different markets

Manufacturer warranties are usually handled by the local distributor, and a lens imported from another region may not be covered where you live. Check before you buy from an overseas seller.

Your rights against the seller are separate from the warranty, and they vary:

- **UK:** under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, you have 30 days to reject a faulty item for a refund. After that, the seller must first offer a repair or replacement.
- **EU:** sellers must give at least a two-year legal guarantee. A defect that shows up in the first year is presumed to have been there at delivery.
- **Australia:** consumer guarantees under the Australian Consumer Law apply on top of any warranty and have no fixed end date. They last for a reasonable time for the product.
- **Singapore:** under the Lemon Law, a defect reported within six months of delivery is presumed to have been there, unless the seller proves otherwise.
- **US:** there is no national legal guarantee like these. You rely mainly on the manufacturer's warranty, the retailer's return policy and state law.

These rights cover buying from a business. A private second-hand sale gives you much less protection, which matters with used lenses and adapters. Our [deals page](/deals) shows prices from retailers in each market.
