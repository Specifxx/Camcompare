A grey-market camera is a genuine product sold outside the maker's official channel for your country. It is usually cheaper, and it is usually the same camera. The difference is what happens when something goes wrong, and that depends on the brand, the seller and where you live.

## What grey market means

Camera makers sell through a local arm or an appointed distributor in each country. That company imports the stock, supplies authorised dealers, runs the local warranty and handles repairs.

A grey import, or parallel import, is stock that reached the shop by another route. A trader buys cameras or lenses where they are cheaper, often in another country, and sells them where they cost more. In the US it is spelled "gray market".

Grey-market goods are:

- **Genuine.** They are made by or for the maker. A counterfeit is a different problem.
- **Usually new.** Grey stock can also be open-box or used. The term describes the sales channel, not the condition.
- **Usually legal to buy.** Trade mark rules that limit parallel imports mainly target the businesses that import and sell them, not buyers.

Listings rarely say "grey market". Nikon USA, for example, tells buyers to watch for terms such as "Import Model", "Direct Import" and "USA Seller Warranty". Elsewhere you may see "international version", "parallel import" or a store warranty in place of the maker's.

## Why grey prices are lower

The same camera can cost noticeably more in one country than another. Common reasons:

- **Regional pricing.** Makers set prices for each market, and exchange rates move faster than price lists.
- **The local arm's costs.** The official distributor pays for warranty repairs, service centres, support and marketing. An importer avoids most of those costs.

The saving is not always large. Makers' cashback schemes, promotions and bundles can be limited to stock sold through their local channel. Nikon USA, for one, says grey-market items are not eligible for its promotions or instant rebates. That can make local stock the better deal.

## What you give up: warranty and service

### The maker's warranty is often regional

Camera warranties are often run by the local arm or distributor, and some cover only stock sold through that channel. Nikon USA, for example, says grey-market products are not covered by a Nikon USA warranty and are not eligible for its warranty repair service. So a grey import may have no maker's warranty where you live.

Some makers or product lines offer a warranty honoured in several countries. Policies differ by brand, country and product, so read the terms on the maker's local website.

### Repairs after the warranty ends

Out-of-warranty repair can be affected too. Nikon USA sends owners of grey-market products to the seller or importer for repairs. Other makers' policies vary, so if you might need the maker's service centre, ask it before you buy. Independent repairers can often help, though parts for a model never sold locally may take longer to find.

### Store and third-party warranties

Grey sellers often offer their own warranty, or a third-party one, instead. It can be a fair substitute, but check:

- who carries out repairs, and whether you must post the item abroad;
- what happens if the shop or warranty company closes;
- what is excluded, and how long a repair should take.

### Names, plugs and paperwork

The camera is normally identical, but some makers name models differently by market. Canon's EOS Rebel SL3, sold in North America, is the EOS 250D in Europe, the EOS 200D Mark II in Australia and Asia, and the EOS Kiss X10 in Japan. The charger plug may be for another country, and the manual in another language.

## How to spot a grey import

- **The price is well below every authorised dealer.** Nikon USA notes that most grey-market items are sold online at a discount.
- **The wording.** Look for the terms above, or a warranty that names the shop rather than the maker.
- **The seller is not on the maker's list.** Some makers, Nikon USA among them, publish lists of their authorised dealers.
- **It is a marketplace listing.** On Amazon, eBay and similar sites, third-party stock may be grey even when the listing does not say so.
- **The box, plug or warranty card are for another country,** or there is no warranty card at all.

If you are not sure, ask the seller in writing whether the item is local stock covered by the maker's warranty in your country. Keep the answer with your receipt.

## How it differs by market

The maker's warranty aside, two questions matter: may a shop sell grey stock, and what can you claim from it?

| Market | Can shops sell grey stock? | Your rights against the seller | Maker's local warranty |
|---|---|---|---|
| US | Generally yes | No national statutory guarantee; warranty, returns and state law | Often limited to authorised US dealers |
| UK | Yes if first sold in the UK or EEA | Consumer Rights Act 2015 | Often limited to local stock |
| EU | Yes if first sold in the EEA | At least a two-year legal guarantee | Often limited to local stock |
| Australia | Generally yes | Australian Consumer Law guarantees | Often limited to local stock |
| Singapore | Widely sold | Lemon Law | Often limited to local stock |

### United States

Reselling genuine goods made for another market is broadly legal in the US, and grey-market cameras are sold openly. In 2013 the Supreme Court held that copyright's "first sale" rule covers copies lawfully made abroad (Kirtsaeng v. John Wiley & Sons). Trade mark rules are narrower: Customs can stop imports that differ materially from the US version unless they are labelled as not authorised by the US trade mark owner.

The US has no national statutory guarantee like the UK's Consumer Rights Act, the EU's legal guarantee or the Australian Consumer Law. You rely mainly on the written warranty, the shop's return policy and state law. Implied warranties under state law can usually be disclaimed, for example by selling "as is", but under the federal Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act a seller that gives its own written warranty cannot disclaim them.

### United Kingdom and the EU

In the EU, goods first sold anywhere in the European Economic Area by or with the consent of the trade mark owner can move freely between member states. A camera from a dealer in another EU country is not grey for that reason alone. Goods first sold outside the EEA are different: importing them without the trade mark owner's consent can infringe its rights.

The UK recognises goods first sold in the UK or the EEA, and in May 2025 the government decided to keep that approach. Stock first sold elsewhere faces the same trade mark limits as in the EU.

Your rights against a UK or EU shop do not depend on where the stock came from:

- **UK:** under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 you have 30 days to reject a faulty item for a full refund. A fault that appears within six months of delivery is presumed to have been there at delivery, unless the shop shows otherwise; after that, it is usually up to you to show it. You have up to six years to bring a claim in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and five in Scotland.
- **EU:** the seller owes at least a two-year legal guarantee. For a defect that appears in the first year, and in some countries the first two, you do not have to prove it was present at delivery.

These rights run against the seller, not the maker, and a store warranty cannot reduce them. Since Brexit, a maker's UK and EU warranties may be separate, so check whether a camera bought in one is covered in the other.

### Australia

Parallel imports of genuine goods are generally legal in Australia. The maker's Australian arm may decline warranty work on them.

The consumer guarantees in the Australian Consumer Law apply to products bought from an Australian business, local stock or not. Goods must be of acceptable quality for a reasonable time, which depends on the product and its price, and the guarantees can still apply after a warranty has run out.

The shop that sold it must deal with your claim. For a major failure you can choose a refund or a replacement, or keep the item and claim compensation for its drop in value. For a minor one, the shop can choose to repair, replace or refund, and must do so within a reasonable time. A store warranty cannot take those rights away.

### Singapore

Grey-market cameras are widely sold in Singapore. Ask whether a camera is a "local set" from the official distributor or a parallel import, what warranty comes with it, and who carries out repairs.

The Lemon Law gives you rights against the business that sold you a defective item. If a defect shows within six months of delivery, it is presumed to have been there at delivery unless the shop proves otherwise. You can ask first for a repair or replacement, then for a price reduction or refund if that is not possible.

## Buying direct from overseas

Ordering from a seller abroad adds risks:

- **Import charges.** VAT, GST or duty may be charged at checkout or on delivery. Rules change, so check the total first.
- **Returns.** Posting a camera back abroad is slow and costly.
- **Your rights.** Consumer law is usually harder to enforce against a seller abroad.
- **Warranty.** The maker's warranty may apply only in the country of sale.

## Is a grey import worth it?

It depends on the saving and on how you would cope if the camera failed.

A grey import can make sense when the saving is large compared with a likely repair bill, the seller's warranty is clear and backed by a business you can reach, and you are happy to use an independent repairer.

Buy local stock when:

- you rely on the camera for paid work and need fast repairs;
- you want a maker's cashback or promotion;
- the price gap is small, or the seller is vague about the warranty;
- you plan to join a maker's professional support scheme; check its rules on where gear must have been bought.
