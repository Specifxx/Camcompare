A used camera or lens can cost far less than a new one and take exactly the same pictures. It can also arrive with a tired shutter, a hazy lens or no easy way to get your money back. This guide covers where to buy, what to check, and how returns, warranties and payment protection work in the US, UK, EU, Australia and Singapore.

## How much buying used saves

The saving depends on the item, its condition and the timing, so there is no single figure. Some patterns hold:

- **Bodies usually lose value faster than lenses.** Makers replace bodies more often, and a successor's launch tends to lower the older model's used price.
- **Recent releases usually save the least.** A new camera on promotion or with cashback can even cost less than a used one.
- **Where you buy changes the price.** A dealer's graded, warrantied item costs more than the same thing from a private seller; the difference buys inspection and the right to return it.

## Dealers, marketplaces and private sellers

### Specialist dealers and camera shops

Dealers in used gear and camera shops with a used department inspect and grade what they sell, and usually offer a warranty and a returns window. Grading scales differ, so one shop's "excellent" is not another's: read the definitions, and prefer photos of the actual item over stock images. Some camera makers also sell refurbished stock with their own warranty in some markets.

### Online marketplaces

Marketplaces such as eBay usually cost less and offer far more choice. eBay's Money Back Guarantee covers items that don't arrive or don't match the listing, if you pay through eBay's checkout and open a request in time. Check the seller's feedback and returns policy, and ask for photos of the item, its serial number and any fault mentioned. "For parts" or "not working" means exactly that.

### Local private sales

Classified sites and selling apps are usually cheapest, and you can handle the item before paying. Your legal rights are thinnest here and scams are common. Meet somewhere public, bring a memory card and, if you can, your own lens or body, and test everything before you pay.

## What to check on a used camera body

### Shutter count

A mechanical shutter wears out eventually. Makers quote a rated life for some models, but that is an average from testing, not a promise, and a worn shutter can be replaced.

Some brands write the count into each photo's metadata, which free tools can read; others need a service centre or special software. Ask the seller for a recent, unedited JPEG straight from the camera. The file number is not the shutter count: it rolls over and can be reset.

Many mirrorless cameras also have an electronic shutter, which doesn't wear the mechanical one, and some, such as Nikon's Z9, have no mechanical shutter at all.

### Sensor

Take a photo with the lens cap on and look at it enlarged for bright dots, which are hot or stuck pixels. A few on long exposures are common, and some cameras have a pixel-mapping function that hides them. Then photograph a plain sky or white wall at f/16 to show dust. Dust can be cleaned; scratches on the sensor's cover glass can't be polished out.

On some bodies with in-body stabilisation, the sensor can move slightly when the camera is off, so a faint rattle can be normal; check the manual. Also check it hasn't been converted for infrared or astrophotography, which gives ordinary photos odd colour.

### Mount, screen, battery and firmware

- **Mount:** deep scratches, a bent mount or missing screws can point to a drop. The contacts should be clean, and a lens should lock on without wobbling.
- **Screen and viewfinder:** look for scratches, dead pixels and dust. Check the touchscreen responds everywhere and a tilting or vari-angle screen moves smoothly.
- **Battery:** batteries age. Some bodies, including many Canon and Nikon models, show battery health in a battery-information menu. Don't use a swollen battery.
- **Firmware:** compare the version in the menu with the latest on the maker's support site. If the menus offer only a few languages, the camera may have been made for another region, which can affect warranty cover.
- **Everything else:** work every button and dial, and look for green or white corrosion on the battery and card contacts, a common sign of water damage. Check the serial number matches the box and receipt, if the seller has them.

## What to check on a used lens

### Fungus, haze and separation

Shine a bright torch through the lens from both ends, looking at an angle, with the aperture as wide open as the lens allows.

- **Fungus** looks like thin threads or a spider's web, often starting at the edge of an element. It grows in warm, damp, dark storage, can spread and can etch the coatings. Avoid a lens with fungus unless the price allows for a professional clean.
- **Haze** is an even, milky fog across an element. It lowers contrast and isn't always removable.
- **Separation**, mostly in older lenses, shows as a rainbow-coloured or white patch near the edge, where cemented elements have come apart.
- **Dust:** a few specks inside are normal and rarely show in photos.

### Glass, focus and fit

Small marks on the front element usually have little visible effect; marks on the rear element are more likely to show. Autofocus should be quick and consistent near and far. The zoom and focus rings should turn evenly, without grinding. Stabilisation, if fitted, should settle the view when you half-press the shutter.

Photograph a flat, detailed subject such as a brick wall, square on, wide open and stopped down. If one side is clearly softer than the other, the lens may be decentred, often after a knock.

Check the lens is made for your mount, not just your brand: some lenses come in several mounts, and an adapted lens can lose autofocus speed or features. [Mounts](/mounts) shows which lenses fit which bodies.

## Returns, warranties and payment protection

**Test at once.** Run the checks above as soon as the item arrives, inside any returns window, and keep the packaging until you're sure.

**Read the warranty.** Find out who repairs the item, what's excluded (wear, accidental damage and batteries often are) and who pays postage. Any maker's warranty still left may need the original proof of purchase and may only cover the country of sale. A grey or parallel import may not be covered by the local maker at all.

**Pay safely:**

- Pay through the marketplace's own checkout, since its guarantee usually depends on it. A seller who wants to move the deal off the platform is a warning sign.
- Credit cards usually give the strongest fallback, through the card scheme's chargeback process and, in the US and UK, specific laws.
- Avoid bank transfers, gift cards, crypto and "friends and family" payments with strangers: that money is hard or impossible to get back. PayPal's Buyer Protection doesn't cover personal payments.
- A price far below the going rate, with no receipt and a seller in a hurry, is a common sign of stolen or counterfeit goods. If gear turns out to be stolen, you can lose it and be left chasing the seller.

## Market notes

These are the general rules for buying from a business. Private sales get much less protection everywhere.

| Market | Buying used from a business | Online change of mind |
|---|---|---|
| US | Seller's warranty; an implied warranty that it works, unless sold "as is" where state law allows | Seller's policy |
| UK | Satisfactory quality, as described and fit for purpose (Consumer Rights Act 2015) | 14 days after delivery |
| EU | 2-year legal guarantee; some countries allow 1 year for used goods if agreed | 14 days from delivery |
| Australia | Consumer guarantees, allowing for the item's age and price | Seller's policy |
| Singapore | Lemon Law: defects reported within six months presumed present at delivery | Seller's policy |

### United States

Returns depend on the seller: there is no general federal right to return an online purchase. To dispute a credit card charge for goods not delivered as agreed, your letter should reach the issuer within 60 days of the first statement showing it. Disputes about quality have extra conditions under federal rules.

### United Kingdom

From a trader, satisfactory quality is judged against the item's description and price. You have 30 days to reject a faulty item for a refund, and a fault in the first six months is presumed present at delivery unless the trader proves otherwise. From a private seller, the item must be as described, but the satisfactory-quality rule doesn't apply.

Section 75 makes your credit card provider jointly liable with the seller for single items costing more than £100 and up to £30,000. Debit cards have chargeback.

### European Union

If a defect appears in the first year (two in some countries), the professional seller must prove it wasn't there at delivery. With the 14-day withdrawal right you usually pay the return postage. Neither the guarantee nor the withdrawal right covers goods from private individuals, and online marketplaces must tell you whether a seller is a trader. In both the EU and the UK, a business posing as a private seller is breaking the law.

### Australia

For a major problem with used goods from a business, you can choose a refund or a replacement. There is no legal right to a change-of-mind refund. Most guarantees don't apply to one-off private sales or traditional auctions, though the guarantee of clear title does.

### Singapore

Under the Lemon Law, part of the Consumer Protection (Fair Trading) Act, you can ask a business seller to repair or replace goods that were defective at delivery, and if neither is possible, to cut the price or refund you. It doesn't cover private sales. The Consumers Association of Singapore (CASE) can help with negotiation or mediation.

Ask whether a body is a local set or a parallel import, because the maker's local warranty may not cover a parallel import. The humid climate also makes fungus checks especially important, so ask how a lens has been stored.
