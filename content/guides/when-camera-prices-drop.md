Camera and lens prices rarely drift down smoothly. They tend to move in steps: when a maker runs a promotion, when a newer model arrives, or when a big sale season starts. Knowing those patterns, and checking a product's price history before you buy, is the most reliable way to avoid paying more than you need to.

## Maker promotions: instant savings and cashback

Many of the biggest drops on new gear are funded by the maker, not the shop, and they last for a set period.

### Instant savings in the US

In the US, makers' promotions usually appear as instant savings: the discount comes off the price at authorised dealers for a fixed period, with no claim to make. When the period ends, the price goes back up, often overnight. Some promotions cover a body only when bought with a particular lens, or a lens only when bought with a body.

### Cashback in the UK, EU and Australia

In the UK, the EU and Australia, makers often run cashback instead. You pay the full shelf price, then claim money back from the maker. Fujifilm UK's lens cashback for June and July 2026 shows the typical terms:

- the purchase had to be from a listed qualifying retailer in the UK;
- only new products counted, not second-hand or refurbished ones;
- claims could be made no sooner than four weeks after purchase, and before a fixed closing date;
- you needed an invoice or receipt (an order confirmation was not enough) and a photo of the serial number;
- returning the product voided the claim.

Payment can take several weeks after a claim is approved. Some schemes pay out only if you also buy a second item, such as a battery grip, and some promotions offer an extended warranty instead of cash.

### Before you count on a promotion

- **Read the dates.** Most schemes have a purchase window and a separate claim deadline.
- **Check where you buy.** Promotions are usually limited to local stock from authorised dealers. Grey imports and marketplace sellers often don't qualify.
- **Keep the paperwork.** You may need the invoice, the serial number and the box.
- **Work out the net price.** Our prices are the retailer's price on the day. A cashback you claim later isn't subtracted.

## Minimum advertised pricing in the US

Many camera makers in the US have a minimum advertised price (MAP) policy: the lowest price an authorised dealer may advertise. It doesn't strictly fix the price you pay, which is why some shops show "add to cart for price" or offer a lower price at checkout.

The US Federal Trade Commission explains that a manufacturer may choose to deal only with retailers who follow its resale price policy, and may stop supplying those that don't. Since a 2007 Supreme Court decision, federal law judges minimum resale price rules case by case rather than banning them outright, though some state laws take a stricter view.

For buyers, MAP has two effects:

- **Authorised dealers tend to show the same price,** so shopping around between them saves less than you might expect.
- **Drops happen together.** When a maker announces instant savings, the advertised price falls at every authorised dealer at once, and rises again when the promotion ends.

A listing well below the common price is often a grey import, an open-box or used item, or a marketplace seller not bound by the policy. That can still be a good buy, but check what you are getting.

The UK, the EU and Australia take a different view. Competition law there generally forbids a maker from setting the minimum price a shop sells at, though makers may recommend a price. The European Commission's guidelines also treat minimum advertised prices as an indirect way of fixing resale prices. Shops in those markets set their own prices, so a drop at one doesn't have to appear at another.

## When a successor launches

### The older model, new

When a maker replaces a camera or lens, the outgoing model is often discounted or put on promotion to clear stock. Once that stock runs out, new copies can become scarce, or come mainly from grey or marketplace sellers. Some makers keep the older model on sale alongside the new one, usually at a lower price. Our product pages say when a model is discontinued and new stock may be limited.

### The older model, used

A launch also tends to bring more used copies onto the market, as owners sell to fund the upgrade, and more supply usually pushes the used price down. The effect is usually clearer for bodies than for lenses, which makers replace less often.

It doesn't always work that way. When a camera is hard to find, used prices can rise instead: at the height of its popularity, the Fujifilm X100V sold on some sites for more than its original launch price.

### The new model

A newly launched camera is rarely discounted in its first months, and popular models can be on back order. The first discounts often arrive with the maker's first promotion on it or with a sale season. If you don't need the newest features, the previous model at a reduced price is often better value. Use [compare](/compare) to see what the newer one actually adds.

## Sale seasons

Sale seasons are when makers' promotions and shops' own discounts tend to overlap.

| Market | Sale periods to watch |
|---|---|
| US | Black Friday and Cyber Monday (27 and 30 November 2026), year-end holiday promotions, Amazon's Prime events |
| UK | Black Friday, Boxing Day (26 December) and January sales, Amazon's Prime events |
| EU | Black Friday, winter and summer sales (set by law in some countries, such as France), Amazon's Prime events where Amazon has a local store |
| Australia | End of financial year (EOFY) sales in June, Black Friday, Boxing Day |
| Singapore | Marketplace "double-date" sales, especially 11.11 (11 November), also 9.9 and 12.12; Black Friday; Amazon's Prime events |

Amazon's Prime Day has been held in June or July in recent years, with a second Prime sale in October. Australia's financial year ends on 30 June, which is why many shops there run EOFY sales through June.

A sale label alone tells you little. Check two things:

- **Was the price higher just before?** Some "deals" match a price that was available weeks earlier. In the EU, a shop that announces a price reduction must show the lowest price it charged in the previous 30 days. Consumer law in the other markets also forbids misleading "was" prices, but you can't count on the same 30-day test, so check the history yourself.
- **Whose discount is it?** If it is the maker's, every authorised dealer will usually have it. If it is one shop's, it may sell out.

## Why the lowest price is often used or refurbished

On many product pages, the lowest price is not for a new copy:

- **Open-box and ex-demo stock** is new or nearly new gear that has been unpacked, returned or used on a shop counter.
- **Refurbished** gear has been checked and repaired, by the maker or by a dealer. A maker's refurbished stock usually carries its warranty, sometimes for a shorter period than new.
- **Used** gear has already taken the fall in value that comes with a successor's launch, so its price tends to move less afterwards.

Who sells it matters as much as the price. Consumer law protects you when you buy from a business; a private sale gives you far fewer rights in all five of our markets. In the UK, second-hand goods from a business must still be of satisfactory quality, allowing for age and price. Some EU countries let a seller of second-hand goods agree a shorter liability period with you, but not under one year. In Australia, the consumer guarantees apply to second-hand goods sold by a business, allowing for age and condition.

Cashback schemes usually exclude used and refurbished stock, so during a promotion a new copy can cost less than a used one.

Body-and-lens kits appear in the offers table but don't count towards a body's lowest price, and out-of-stock offers are left out. The Save button keeps a product in a list in your browser and shows how its price has changed since you saved it.

### Reading the chart

- **A flat line with sudden steps** usually means a maker's promotion started or ended. Promotions often recur, though not on a fixed schedule.
- **A gradual slide in the used line** often follows a successor's launch.
- **The used line is noisier.** It follows whichever used listing is cheapest that day, and condition varies.
- **A one-day dip** may be a single marketplace seller. Check the offers table for the seller, the condition and whether the shop is an authorised dealer.

## A simple plan

1. Narrow the field with the [finder](/finder), and check which lenses fit on [mounts](/mounts).
2. Open the product page in your market and read the chart and the deal note.
3. If a successor has just been announced or a sale season is weeks away, waiting may pay. If the price is already at its recorded low, it may not.
4. Compare the best new price, after any cashback, with the best used or refurbished one, and weigh the warranty and the seller.
