Full frame is often presented as the upgrade every serious photographer should make, and APS-C as the budget compromise. The reality is more balanced: a full-frame sensor gives you real advantages in light and background blur, while APS-C gives you reach and smaller, often cheaper lenses. This guide sets out what changes between the two, with real examples, so you can decide which matters for what you shoot.

## The two formats in brief

A full-frame sensor measures about 36 × 24 mm. Most APS-C sensors measure about 23.5 × 15.6 mm, a crop factor of about 1.5; Canon's are a little smaller, with a crop factor of 1.6. Full frame has roughly 2.4 times the area of the common APS-C size. [Camera sensor sizes explained](/guides/sensor-sizes-explained) covers all the formats and the arithmetic behind crop factors.

That difference in area drives almost everything below.

## What full frame gains you

### More light, less visible noise

At the same f-number, shutter speed and ISO, both formats give the same exposure. But the full-frame sensor collects about 2.4 times as much light in total, a little over one stop more, so its pictures show less noise when both are viewed at the same size.

In practice, that stop shows up in dim interiors, evening events and high-ISO sport. It can be narrowed: a newer APS-C sensor can beat an older full-frame one, and a brighter lens on APS-C recovers some of the gap. [How to judge a camera's low-light performance](/guides/low-light-performance-explained) shows how to compare specific models rather than formats.

### Shallower depth of field

For the same framing and f-number, full frame gives shallower depth of field. To find the full-frame equivalent of an APS-C lens, multiply both the focal length and the f-number by the crop factor.

A Sigma 56mm f/1.4 on a 1.5× APS-C body frames like an 84mm lens on full frame and gives the depth of field of about f/2.1. A full-frame 85mm f/1.8 is close to that, a little shallower. If you want very blurred backgrounds, full frame gets there with a wider choice of lenses; on APS-C you need the fastest lenses in the range.

### Dynamic range, often

Full-frame sensors often measure a little more dynamic range than APS-C sensors of the same generation, for the same reason they show less noise. This isn't guaranteed: sensor design matters, so check measurements for specific cameras.

### Wide-angle choice

Because APS-C crops the view, you need shorter focal lengths to go wide. A full-frame 16mm lens gives a view that an APS-C body needs about a 10–11mm lens to match. Ultra-wide options exist for APS-C, but full frame has more of them, including fast ones.

## What APS-C gains you

### Reach for the same lens

An APS-C sensor records only the central part of the image a lens projects. A 400mm lens on a 1.5× body frames like 600mm on full frame. If the APS-C sensor has a similar pixel count to the full-frame one, a distant bird or player covers more pixels, which is a real advantage for wildlife and sport on a budget.

A high-resolution full-frame body can crop to match, but you then use only part of its sensor, and the light advantage goes with it.

### Smaller, lighter and often cheaper lenses

Lenses designed only for APS-C can be smaller because they cover a smaller image circle. The Sigma 56mm f/1.4 DC DN for Sony E weighs about 280 g; Sony's full-frame FE 85mm f/1.8 weighs 371 g. The gap is wider for standard zooms and widest for compact kit lenses, where APS-C bodies can be paired with very small zooms.

The advantage shrinks when you want full-frame-like blur, because an APS-C lens then has to be faster, and faster lenses are bigger.

### Deeper depth of field, when you want it

Deeper depth of field is not always a drawback. Landscapes, group shots, macro and solo video are all easier when more of the scene is sharp. On APS-C you reach a given depth of field at a wider aperture, so you can use a faster shutter speed or lower ISO than full frame would need for the same depth of field.

### Cost of entry

APS-C bodies and their lenses usually start lower in price than full-frame ones. Check today's prices on [cameras](/cameras) and [lenses](/lenses), where each product shows its lowest new and used price in your market.

## The comparison at a glance

| | Full frame | APS-C |
|---|---|---|
| Sensor size | About 36 × 24 mm | About 23.5 × 15.6 mm (Canon 22.3 × 14.9 mm) |
| Light at the same settings | About one stop more | Baseline |
| Depth of field at the same framing and f-number | Shallower | Deeper (multiply f-number by 1.5–1.6 to compare) |
| Telephoto reach from a given lens | Baseline | About 1.5× (Canon 1.6×) narrower view |
| Lens size for the same view and brightness | Larger | Smaller |
| Ultra-wide and fast-lens choice | Wider | Narrower in most systems |
| Entry price | Usually higher | Usually lower |

## System choice, brand by brand

The format you pick is also a mount decision, because lenses tend to outlast bodies. The main systems handle the two formats differently.

- **Canon:** the RF mount covers both. APS-C bodies take full-frame RF lenses as well as smaller RF-S lenses. A full-frame RF body can use RF-S lenses with a crop.
- **Nikon:** the Z mount covers both, with DX lenses for APS-C. Full-frame Z lenses work on DX bodies, and DX lenses work on full-frame bodies in a crop mode.
- **Sony:** the E mount covers both. FE lenses are full frame and work on APS-C bodies; APS-C E lenses work on full-frame bodies in a crop mode.
- **Fujifilm:** the X mount is APS-C only. Fujifilm doesn't make full-frame cameras; its larger GFX system is medium format on a separate mount.
- **Pentax:** the K mount has APS-C bodies and the full-frame K-1 series, and many K-mount lenses cover full frame.

A shared mount lets you start on APS-C, buy full-frame lenses as you go, and move up later without replacing them. Using APS-C lenses on a full-frame body works, but in crop mode you lose pixels and the light advantage, so it is a stopgap rather than a plan. ## Used-market prices

Full-frame bodies have been on sale for many years in every major mirrorless system, so the used market is well supplied with earlier generations. That can bring a used full-frame body within reach of a new APS-C one. Before treating that as a bargain, check:

- **The lenses.** A cheap body is no saving if the full-frame lenses you want cost much more than their APS-C equivalents.
- **What the newer APS-C body adds.** Autofocus tracking, video modes and stabilisation have moved on; a recent APS-C model can outperform an older full-frame one in these.
- **Condition and support.** An older body has more wear and fewer years of maker repairs left. [How to buy a used camera or lens safely](/guides/buying-used-cameras) has a checklist.

## Which to choose

There is no format that is better for everyone. These rules of thumb follow from the differences above.

- **Choose full frame** if you shoot often in low light, want very shallow depth of field for portraits, work with ultra-wide lenses, or need the most dynamic range.
- **Choose APS-C** if you want a lighter kit for travel, need telephoto reach for wildlife or sport on a budget, prefer deeper depth of field, or want a lower cost of entry.
- **Choose the mount first** if you are unsure. Canon RF, Nikon Z and Sony E all let you change format later without changing system.
